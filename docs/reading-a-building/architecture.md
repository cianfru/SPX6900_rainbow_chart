# Architecture

**Glass towers represent the city's largest holders.**

**Concrete mid-rises occupy the next tier.**

**Brick buildings form the majority of the city.**

The architecture isn't aesthetic. It gives scale immediate visual meaning before the user ever
hovers over a building.

## The four types

Type follows directly from [height](height.md). There is no separate rule.

| Height | Type | Material | Share of the city today |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **≥ 11** | Glass tower, setback top, spire on some | glass | **86** (1.8%) |
| **6 – 11** | Concrete mid-rise with a capped roof | concrete | **406** (8.3%) |
| **3.5 – 6** | Masonry block with a parapet | brick | **1,308** (26.7%) |
| **< 3.5** | Low-rise, wider than deep | brick | **3,093** (63.2%) |

Two thirds of the city is low-rise brick. That is not a styling choice — it is what the distribution
of holdings looks like when you draw it honestly.

## Why the brick matters

The warm masonry mass is load-bearing, not decoration. It is what gives the eye somewhere to rest,
and what makes the towers read as tall in the first place.

A city where everything is glass has no skyline.

Roofs carry water towers, HVAC plant and parapets — the detail that says New York, and nearly free
once the geometry is merged into shared meshes.

{% hint style="info" %}
The thresholds above are calibrated to the height curve and must be retuned **with** it, never
alone. Left unchanged when the curve last moved, every building in the city turned to glass.
{% endhint %}
